# Chapter 5: Kestrel Pass

They did not sail after all. Haskett's guards had chained the harbor mouth during the night, and no ship was allowed in or out without the new governor's seal. So they rode.

Orrin hired three horses from a stable outside the city wall. He rode badly, swearing at every jolt, with his wooden peg strapped into a special stirrup. Tobias had never been on a horse in his life and fell off twice before they reached the coast road. Wren rode in front, with her short black hair blowing in her eyes and her courier's satchel over her shoulder.

Kestrel Pass was half a day's ride south of Veyle. They left at dawn and reached the foot of the mountains a little after noon, where the road turned away from the sea and began to climb between grey cliffs. Kestrels hung in the wind above them, which was how the Pass had gotten its name.

"The watchtower is at the top," Orrin said, looking up. "An hour's climb. We leave the horses here."

They left the horses tied in a stand of pines and went up on foot. Orrin was slow on the steep path, and he cursed his peg leg the whole way, but he did not stop.

---

The watchtower was a broken stone ring on a ledge above the road. Half of it had fallen down the cliff long ago. The other half still had a roof, and smoke was rising from a hole in it.

Wren crept up to a gap in the wall and looked inside. Three men were sitting around a fire. One of them had a lantern next to him with a grey hand painted on the glass. And on a flat stone beside the fire, still half wrapped in sailcloth, was a circlet of plain silver.

She slid back down to where Orrin and Tobias were waiting.

"It's there," she whispered. "Three of them. One has a crossbow."

"Then we need them to come out," Orrin said. "Tobias. Can you make a noise like a whole lot of King's men?"

Tobias grinned.

A minute later, from the rocks on the far side of the tower, came the sound of shouting, boots, and a horn. It was only Tobias, a stolen tin whistle, and a lot of rocks rolled down the slope, but it was enough. Two of the Grey Hand ran out of the tower with their swords drawn and went the wrong way down the hill.

The third one stayed.

Wren went in through the gap. The last man was standing over the crown with the crossbow in his hands, and he swung it toward her. She drew her horn-handled knife with her right hand. Wren had always been right-handed, and her right hand was quick and sure. She threw the knife the way her father had taught her, and it hit the crossbow stock just as the man pulled the trigger. The bolt went into the roof.

Then Orrin came through the gap behind her and hit the man over the head with a piece of firewood, and it was over.

---

They tied the man up with his own belt. Wren worked her knife out of the crossbow stock and put it back on her hip. Then she picked up the crown.

It was lighter than she had expected. The silver was dull and scratched and the pearls were small and yellow, and it looked like the kind of thing you would find at the bottom of a sea chest and not think twice about. She turned it over in her hands. On the inside of the band, someone had engraved a single word in the old coast script.

"What does it say?" Tobias asked. He had come back up the slope, breathless and very pleased with himself.

"Home," said Orrin. He knew a little of the old script. "It says home."

The man they had tied up started to laugh.

"You think you've won," he said. "Take it back to Veyle, then. Take it right to the governor's hall. Lord Haskett will be so glad to see it."

Wren and Orrin looked at each other.

"He's not the governor," Wren said. "Not really. Not until he wears the crown on Founding Day."

"Then we'd better not let him," Orrin said.

---

They rode back toward Veyle in the evening light, with the crown wrapped in Wren's satchel and the Grey Hand man tied over the back of Tobias's horse. Tobias walked beside it, leading it, and did not complain once, which was a new record.

Wren rode in front again. She thought about Lady Seraphine in a cell under the governor's hall. She thought about Lord Haskett, governor of Veyle, standing at the bottom of the hill with his hands raised. She thought about Mira and the dye and the drawing on the fish market door, and about her father's bone-handled dagger lying somewhere at the bottom of the harbor in forty feet of green-black water.

She was a courier. She carried things where they needed to go. That was all this was, really. A delivery.

She touched the horn handle at her hip and urged her horse a little faster toward the city.
