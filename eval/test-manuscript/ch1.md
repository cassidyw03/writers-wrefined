# Chapter 1: The Courier of Veyle

The bells of Veyle rang the sixth hour as Wren Ashby ran down Tallow Street with a sealed letter tucked inside her coat. Veyle was a port city on the eastern coast of the kingdom, and every morning the sun rose out of the sea and turned the harbor the color of brass. The whole city smelled of salt, tar, and fish guts, and Wren had loved that smell for all nineteen years of her life.

She was a courier for the Harbor Guild, which meant she was paid two coppers a letter to run messages between the ships and the counting houses. She was quick, she knew every alley between the fish market and the governor's hill, and she never read what she carried. Mostly.

Wren had red hair that she kept tied back with a strip of sailcloth, and grey eyes that her father used to say were the exact color of a winter sea. She was left-handed, and she wore her father's dagger on her right hip so that her left hand could reach it across her body. The dagger had a bone handle carved with a leaping fish. Her father had given it to her the week before his boat went down in a spring storm, and she had not taken it off since.

She ducked under a line of drying nets and came out by her sister's infirmary. Mira Ashby was Wren's older sister, four years older, and the only healer on Tallow Street who would treat a sailor who could not pay. Mira was standing in the doorway with her sleeves rolled up and blood on her apron.

"You're late," Mira said.

"I'm early. The Guild hasn't even opened."

"Then you're early for the wrong thing. Soot was in the herb cupboard again."

Soot was their cat, a fat grey tom with one torn ear who belonged to nobody and slept wherever he liked. At the moment he was sitting on the infirmary windowsill, cleaning a paw and looking guilty about nothing.

Their mother had died of the river fever when Wren was six years old, and after their father drowned, Mira had raised her. Mira still talked to her like she was six. Wren had mostly stopped minding.

"Who's the letter for?" Mira asked.

"I don't read them."

"Who's the letter for, Wren?"

Wren sighed. "Captain Orrin Vael. The seal is the governor's."

Mira's eyebrows went up. Everyone in Veyle knew Lady Seraphine Dorr's seal, a silver wave on black wax. Lady Seraphine had been governor of Veyle for eleven years. She was a tall woman with silver hair that she wore in a crown of braids, and she almost never sent letters to sea captains, especially not to old smugglers like Orrin Vael.

"Be careful," Mira said.

"It's a letter."

"It's a letter from the governor to a pirate."

"He's not a pirate anymore. He's retired." Wren grinned and ran before Mira could argue.

The *Gull's Lament* was tied up at the end of the long pier, where the water was deepest. She was an old ship with patched sails and a carved gull on her prow whose paint had mostly peeled away. Captain Orrin Vael was sitting on an upturned barrel on her deck, mending a rope.

Orrin was sixty years old and looked older. His beard was white and his face was brown and creased like an old boot. He had lost his left leg below the knee to a shark off the Teeth thirty years ago, and he walked on a wooden peg that thumped on the deck boards. He told a different story about the shark every time someone asked. In Wren's favorite version, he had punched the shark in the eye and it had apologized.

"Wren Ashby," he said without looking up. "What trouble are you bringing me today?"

"Governor's seal." She held up the letter.

Orrin stopped mending. He wiped his hands on his trousers and took the letter, turned it over, and studied the silver wave in the black wax for a long moment. Then he broke the seal and read.

Wren watched his face. Orrin had been a smuggler for most of his life and he did not give much away, but she saw his jaw tighten. He read the letter twice. Then he folded it and put it inside his shirt.

"Sit down," he said.

"I have other letters."

"Sit down, girl."

She sat on a coil of rope. Orrin looked out over the harbor for a while before he spoke.

"Three nights ago," he said quietly, "someone broke into the governor's vault and stole the Salt Crown."

Wren stared at him. The Salt Crown was the oldest treasure in Veyle. It was kept in the vault beneath the governor's hall and brought out once a year, on Founding Day, when the governor wore it to bless the fishing fleet. Wren had seen it once, from a distance, when she was small. She remembered it shining.

"Who would steal the Salt Crown? You couldn't sell it. Everyone would know what it was."

"That," Orrin said, "is what the governor would like to know. She wants me to find it before the Council finds out it's gone." He looked at her sideways. "And she wants a courier who knows the city and can keep her mouth shut."

"Me?"

"You read the letter, didn't you?"

"I did not!"

"Then how do you know it was about you?"

Wren opened her mouth and closed it again. Orrin laughed, a big rough laugh that made the gulls on the rail take off.

"We start tomorrow," he said. "Bring your father's knife."
