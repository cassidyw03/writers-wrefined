# Chapter 2: The Drowned Library

The Drowned Library was under the harbor. Long ago, before the sea wall was built, the old city of Veyle had flooded, and the library of the Lantern Order had gone under with it. The monks had sealed the doors with pitch, pumped the water out, and kept on working down there in the dark, and they had been working there ever since.

Orrin led Wren down a stone stair behind the customs house. The walls sweated with seawater and the air was cold and thick with the smell of old paper and lamp oil. Every few steps a brass lantern hung from a hook, which was how the Lantern Order had gotten its name.

At the bottom was a long, low room full of shelves, and at the end of the room, at a desk covered in maps, sat a thin old man in a grey robe.

"Brother Edric," Orrin said. "I've brought the courier."

Brother Edric turned his face toward them. His eyes were pale and clouded, and Wren realized that he was blind. Orrin had told her on the way that Edric had been blind since birth, and that he was the keeper of maps for the Lantern Order anyway, because he could read the raised ink on the old charts with his fingertips faster than most men could read with their eyes.

"The courier," Edric said. "Wren Ashby. Your sister set my wrist last winter. Tell her it still aches when it rains."

"I'll tell her," Wren said, surprised.

"Sit. Both of you. Orrin tells me the governor has lost her crown."

They sat on a bench across from him. Edric folded his long hands on the desk.

"The Salt Crown," he said, "was made for the first queen of the coast, four hundred years ago. It is a circlet of plain silver, set with three pearls. The three pearls are for the three gifts of the sea: food, trade, and the dead returned. It is not worth very much as silver. The pearls are old and yellow. But it is the oldest thing in Veyle, and whoever wears it on Founding Day speaks for the city."

"So whoever stole it doesn't want to sell it," Wren said slowly. "They want to wear it."

Edric smiled. "Your sister said you were clever."

"My sister says I'm reckless."

"Those are often the same thing."

Orrin grunted. "If someone means to wear it, they'll need to keep it safe until Founding Day. That's six weeks. They'll have taken it out of the city."

"I have heard," said Edric, "that a man with a heavy chest bought passage on a fishing boat four nights ago and asked to be put ashore north of the sea wall. I don't know more than that. But I know who might."

---

The person who might know more turned out to be a boy named Tobias Fenn. He was fourteen years old, skinny and sharp-faced, and he ran with the pickpockets who worked the fish market. Brother Edric said that Tobias had been seen near the governor's hall the night the crown was stolen.

Orrin went back to the *Gull's Lament* to ready her for sea. She was a two-masted ketch, small and quick, and Orrin swore she could outrun anything in the harbor if the wind was right. Wren went to the fish market alone to find Tobias.

She found him faster than she expected, because he found her first. She was standing at a stall asking about him when she felt a light touch at her hip. She spun around and caught a boy by the wrist. He had her father's bone-handled dagger in his hand.

Tobias stared into her green eyes and grinned. "Sorry, miss. Thought you dropped it."

Then he twisted free and ran.

Wren ran after him. He went over a fish cart, under a table of crabs, and down the steps to the lower pier, and she was right behind him the whole way. At the end of the pier he had nowhere to go. He turned around, saw her coming, and threw the dagger as hard as he could out over the water.

Wren watched it spin, flash once in the sun, and disappear into the harbor.

She stood at the edge of the pier for a long time. The water there was forty feet deep and green-black with silt. Her father's dagger was gone. It was never coming back.

When she turned around, Tobias was still there. He looked a little sick.

"I didn't think you'd care that much," he said. "It's just a knife."

"It was my father's."

Tobias was quiet. Then he said, "I know who took the crown. I'll tell you. To make it even."

---

That evening, Wren bought a new knife from a cutler's stall at the edge of the market with the last of her month's coppers. It was plain steel with a handle of brown horn, and it was not her father's, and it never would be. She hung it on her hip where the old dagger had been.

Then she went to tell Mira what had happened. Her little sister Mira was three years younger than Wren, but she had always been the sensible one. Mira listened to the whole story without interrupting, washed and bandaged the scrape Wren had gotten going over the fish cart, and then hugged her for a long time.

"Father would say it's only a knife," Mira said.

"Father would have gone in after it."

"Yes," Mira said. "He would have. And then I would have had to fish both of you out."

Soot jumped into Wren's lap and went to sleep there, which was the closest thing to sympathy he ever offered.
